# Decision log

Non-obvious choices, in roughly the order they were made.

1. **Brand = SpotifyCares, not AmazonHelp or AppleSupport.** Profiling (`scripts/00_profile_brands.py`) showed AmazonHelp is multilingual and mostly sends a boilerplate "reach us via this link" reply. AppleSupport sends 52% of replies to DM. Spotify is English, has eight distinct issue types, and solves many issues in public, so the agent has real resolution knowledge to learn from.
2. **The unit is "thread-opening customer tweet → first brand reply".** Multi-turn follow-ups are out of scope (see *What I chose not to build*). This keeps one decision per message and makes labels unambiguous.
3. **Time-based split at 2017-11-25.** The knowledge base (34k pairs) is strictly before the cutoff and the golden set strictly after it. A random split would let retrieval surface near-duplicates of the test tweet, sometimes the very same reply being graded. Cost: the test window contains an outage that the KB never saw (see failure analysis).
4. **Intents come from what the agent has to *do*, not from topic words.** KMeans on TF-IDF (k=25) gave topical clusters ("99", "student", "album"). I merged them into 8 intents by resolution path. For example, "student discount charged full price" is a money problem and is handled like billing, so tie-break rules are written down in `src/intents.py`.
5. **Hacked accounts are their own intent**, separate from login problems, even though they are rarer. The cost of mixing them up is asymmetric: a "reset your password" reply to a hijacked account is harmful.
6. **The escalation policy was written before labelling (then refined once during the first pass: E4 narrowed to non-English only, since a clarifying question handles vague tweets; E3 widened to sensitive personal circumstances). Labels follow the policy, not history.** Historically Spotify sent ~32% of replies to DM, including simple how-to questions. Copying that would train the agent to over-escalate. The policy (E1 account action, E2 security/PII, E3 high-risk tone, E4 non-English) is the definition of "good".
7. **Golden set = 150 uniform-random + 50 risk-oversampled.** A purely random sample of 150 holds only a handful of security/fraud cases, too few to measure the escalation behaviour that matters most. Metrics are reported on "all 200" and "random 150" separately, because the oversample inflates the escalation share.
8. **One LLM call per message** does intent, escalation and reply together, with retrieved examples in the prompt. Separate calls would triple the cost on a free tier (≈1k requests/day) for no measured gain. The intent and escalation fields act as reasoning the reply has to agree with.
9. **TF-IDF retrieval instead of embeddings.** It needs no API quota and builds in about 2 seconds. Tweets name their issue in plain words. Replies that only say "we've replied to your DM" are capped at one per retrieval: they show *when* to escalate but contain no resolution knowledge.
10. **Deterministic guardrails can only add escalations**: regexes for posted PII (the dataset's `__email__`/`__credit_card__` tokens), hack/compromise words, and a non-English heuristic. A prompt-only policy is not something I'd trust for the security case. `agent_noguard` is reported so the guardrails' effect is visible.
11. **The agent must not invent links or facts.** URLs in the data are dead `t.co` shorteners (replaced by `<URL>`), so the agent writes `[link: …]` placeholders for a human or a link resolver to fill in. The judge's `grounded` score penalises invented facts.
12. **The judge is a different model** (`gemini-3.1-flash-lite`) from the agent (`gemini-3.5-flash-lite`), to reduce self-preference bias. This was forced by quota: I wanted a stronger judge (`gemini-3.8-flash`, then `gemini-2.5-flash`), but both allow only 20 free requests/day. The weaker judge is why judge–human agreement is measured, not assumed. The 19 items `gemini-2.5-flash` had judged are kept as a small check against a stronger judge.
13. **Judging all candidates for a tweet in one call** (5 replies: agent, no-retrieval agent, two baselines, and the real historical reply) keeps the judge to 200 calls, inside the free-tier daily limit. The risk is that the judge scores replies relative to each other. Instead of assuming that away, I measure it: the human ratings are collected one reply at a time.
14. **Headline metric = "safe automation rate"**: the share of *all* messages the agent auto-handles AND that the policy says may be auto-handled AND that the judge rates send-ready. Accuracy alone rewards a system that escalates everything. This metric charges for both unsafe automation and wasted human time.
15. **Every LLM call is cached on disk and the cache is committed.** Reviewers can reproduce every number in about a minute without an API key. Changing a prompt changes the hash, so stale results can't be served by accident.
16. **The simple baseline's keyword rules were written after reading the golden messages.** That is leakage in the baseline's favour. I kept it because it makes the baseline *stronger*, so the comparison is conservative.
